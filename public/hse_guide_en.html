Overview & definitions

Read these few points before completing any questionnaire or running a sampling exercise.

Unlike lagging indicators such as the incident rate, leading (proactive) and intermediate HSE indicators measure the state of safety, health and environment before an incident occurs. Three standard tools are used for this:

HSE Climate — employees' understanding and perception of the HSE climate Accident Proneness — a personality trait of people in critical roles SBS — direct observation of safe and unsafe behavior at the workplace
ℹ️
One important note: HSE climate is not the same as HSE culture. Climate is a psychological, changeable phenomenon that reflects employees' perception at a given point in time; HSE culture is more stable and relates to the organization's shared values.

HSE Climate questionnaire

Measuring employees' perception of safety, health and environment at the workplace

Target group
All personnel and manpower-supply contractors
Completion method
Self-administered, semi-supervised — 43 questions, 5-point scale
Cycle
Annual, in two 6-month rounds (50% of people per round)

Step 1 — Determine how many questionnaires to distribute

The number of questionnaires to print and distribute depends on the company's total headcount, and must be distributed so that every job level (technician, foreman, supervisor, etc.) is represented in the same proportion.

Step 6 — Scheduling and submitting results

  1. Annual distribution in two rounds

    The target group is split in two; every 6 months the questionnaire is handed to 50% of the people.

  2. Data entry

    Immediately after each round of questionnaires is received, the data is recorded and entered into the relevant file.

Assessing the personality trait related to risk-taking — for people in critical roles

Target group
People in "critical roles"
Completion method
Self-administered, semi-supervised — 39 questions, 5-point scale
Cycle
Before hiring / starting work, and once a year for permanently critical roles

Before distributing the questionnaire, a list of "critical roles" must be prepared — meaning roles rated high-risk in the assessments that have been carried out.

Safety Behavior Sampling (SBS)

Field observation and logging of employees' safe/unsafe behavior — the method differs between factories and work sites

4.1 — Sampling in factories

  1. Define the work station

    A work station is one of the company areas where an activity takes place; the factory grounds are divided up on this basis.

  2. Prepare the list of unsafe acts

    Prepared from prior studies and incident reports (disabling injuries, recordable incidents, first-aid cases) and reputable references.

  3. Pilot study

    A pilot study of 200 observations is run to determine the unsafe-behavior ratio (P). Factories that sampled in previous years may use the previous years' unsafe-behavior percentage instead of a pilot.

  4. Calculate the required sample size

    The sample size is calculated with the formula below for a 95% confidence level (K≈1.64); observations are then divided equally among the employees.

  5. Train the observer and run the sampling

    The observer is trained to classify each person's behavior quickly and correctly as "safe" or "unsafe"; sampling is then carried out throughout the year, sensibly distributed across working hours, weekdays and shifts.

Sample-size formula (95% confidence level) N = K² × (1−P) / (S² × P)  →  for 95%: N = 1075 × [(1−P) / P]
Example: if the pilot study found 62 unsafe acts out of 200 observations (P = 31%) and the required precision is S = 5%:
N = (1.64)² × (1−0.31) / (0.05² × 0.31) ≈ 2,392 observations
If the population is 243 people, 2,392 ÷ 243 ≈ 9.8; so 10 observations are made per person (2,430 observations in total). The main study is completed with the remaining (total sample − pilot sample) observations.

4.2 — Sampling at work sites

  1. Prepare the list of unsafe acts

    As with factories, prepared from prior studies and incident reports.

  2. Select eligible work sites

    The selected site must be 20% to 90% complete and must have no fewer than 70 workers.

  3. Pilot study

    Results from previous years' studies may be used instead of a new pilot.

  4. Calculate and allocate the sample size across sites

    The company's total sample size is calculated with the same formula, then divided among the sites in proportion to each site's worker count.

  5. Train the observer and run quarterly

    Sampling is carried out throughout the year at suitable intervals — usually once per quarter, over two to three days.
